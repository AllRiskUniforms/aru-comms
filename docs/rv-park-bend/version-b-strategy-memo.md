# The Constraint Is the Moat
## A contrarian strategy for entering the Bend outdoor-hospitality market
### **Version B: Operator's Strategy Memo**

*Prepared for: All Risk Uniforms / project sponsor*
*Date: August 2026*
*Read this one second. It argues against the obvious plan.*

---

## The thesis in one paragraph

Everyone entering this market is trying to build the same thing: full-hookup pads on commercially zoned land with municipal-grade services. That product requires the scarcest, most expensive, most heavily contested land in Deschutes County, and it is exactly where an under-capitalized entrant is weakest. **The alternative is to build the product the regulations permit on the land nobody else can use — and then let those same regulations keep competitors out.** A hookup-free, low-density camp with a small block of yurts on cheap resource-zoned acreage generates **$0.57 of annual revenue per dollar of capital deployed, against $0.36 for the conventional park** — and it can be built in self-funding phases instead of on a $1.8M construction loan.

---

## Part 1 — Why the obvious plan fails at this capital level

### Wall 1: The capital wall

A 20-pad full-hookup park costs **$780,000–$2,160,000** all-in. That is not a rounding error against a $200,000 budget; it is a different category of project. The gap is closeable with SBA debt (see Version A), but closing it means signing personal guarantees on roughly **$1.8M of construction debt for a first-time operator with no hospitality track record.** That is a real risk, honestly stated, and it deserves to be weighed rather than assumed away.

### Wall 2: The code wall

Here is the rule that reorganizes the entire problem:

> **OAR 660-033-0130(19):** *"Separate sewer, water or electric service hook-ups shall not be provided to individual camp sites"* — except that electrical service may be provided to yurts.

On EFU land — which is where the affordable acreage is — **you cannot build hookups. Not now, not in phase two, not ever.** The "start primitive, upgrade later" plan is not a slow path to a full-hookup park on farm ground. It is a dead end that only reveals itself after the land is bought.

Deschutes County adds its own filter on top: **DCC 18.128.170(O) requires RV park access from an arterial or collector street.** Between the zone requirement and the road requirement, the eligible land pool for a conventional park is small and priced accordingly.

### Wall 3: The scale wall

Even if the capital appears, **20 sites is the wrong number.** The fixed backbone — entitlement, well, septic, power extension, bathhouse — costs the same at 20 pads as at 45. A 20-site park carries a **~64% operating expense ratio** because it still needs a manager, insurance, software and a bathhouse, spread across a third of the revenue. Version A's own model shows a 20-site park building for roughly what it is worth: **no development spread at all.**

**Three walls, one conclusion:** the conventional plan is either a $2M project or it is nothing. If the sponsor wants a $2M project, Version A is the correct document. If not, keep reading.

---

## Part 2 — The reframe

Read OAR 660-033-0130(19) carefully. It bars hookups **to individual campsites**. It says nothing about shared infrastructure.

**Legal on EFU campground land:**
- A central bathhouse with showers and toilets (in fact *required* by Oregon Health Authority rules)
- A centralized potable water fill station
- A central RV dump station
- Electrical service **to yurts** — explicitly carved out by the rule
- Roads, pads, fire rings, common areas

**Not legal:** a water spigot, sewer inlet and power pedestal at each pad.

That is the whole arbitrage. **The guest experience gap between "central dump station and fill station" and "hookups at your pad" is far smaller than the cost gap.** And the cost gap is roughly **$25,000 per site.**

### The moat

The constraint that limits you limits everyone. No competitor can buy the farm ground next door and overbuild you with a 100-pad resort — **the same rule stops them.** A conventional park has to fight for scarce RC/RI land on an arterial; you compete in a land pool an order of magnitude larger and cheaper, with a permanent statutory ceiling on how much competing supply can ever be built beside you.

Conventional developers treat OAR 660-033-0130(19) as the reason they can't use cheap land. It is more usefully read as **the reason nobody can.**

## Part 3 — The product

**30 total campsites on 20± acres of EFU or forest-zoned land.**

| Component | Count | Why |
|---|---:|---|
| Yurts | **10** | The margin engine. Glamping ADR is 3–4x a dry RV pad |
| Dry RV / tent sites | **20** | Volume, low capex, feeds the yurts through repeat guests |
| **Total campsites** | **30** | |

**The 30/10 split is not arbitrary — it is the maximum the statute allows.** Both ORS 215.283 (EFU) and ORS 215.459 (forest) cap yurts at *one-third of campsites or 10, whichever is smaller.* Thirty campsites is the exact point where one-third equals the hard cap of 10. Fewer than 30 sites and you leave yurt revenue on the table; more than 30 and you add low-margin dry sites without earning another yurt.

**Design the site plan at exactly 30 campsites.** That is the revenue-maximizing legal configuration, and it is not obvious from the outside.

Note the statutory definition: a yurt is *"a round, domed shelter of cloth or canvas on a collapsible frame with no plumbing, sewage disposal hookup or internal cooking appliance."* Plumbed, hard-sided cabins are a different use with a different approval path — **do not let a supplier talk you into "yurt-style cabins."** Bathing and cooking happen centrally.

## Part 4 — Site selection filter

The conventional park hunts for RC/RI land on an arterial. This strategy hunts for something else entirely, and the two searches barely overlap.

| Criterion | Requirement | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Zone | EFU or Forest (F1/F2) | The cheap pool. ORS 215.283 / 215.459 authorize private campgrounds |
| Acreage | 20–40 acres | Low density is the product, not a compromise |
| Price | $3,000–$6,000/acre | County undeveloped-land listing average is $5,773/acre |
| Water | Under **5,000 gpd** peak demand | Stays inside the commercial exempt-well ceiling — no water right, no mitigation credits |
| Setting | Adjacent to a recreational resource — river, trailhead, forest boundary, Cascade view | ORS 215.283 case law construes "private park" as **low-intensity outdoor recreation**. The setting is both the product and the legal justification |
| Access | Legal, all-weather, county-approved approach | Confirm the county's access standard for this use class **[VERIFY]** |

**Critical legal note.** Oregon courts have held that a "private park" under ORS 215.283 must be maintained for *natural enjoyment and outdoor recreational use* — a property rented primarily for commercial events fell outside the definition. **Your application must be framed as low-intensity outdoor recreation, and the operation must actually match that framing.** No wedding venue, no event business, no commercial retail beyond incidental camp supplies. Straying from that framing is the most likely way to lose the use on appeal.

Water demand check: 30 sites without per-site plumbing draws far less than a full-hookup park — central bathhouse and fill station only. **Comfortably inside 5,000 gpd**, which removes the single most dangerous line item in Deschutes County development.

## Part 6 — What would falsify this thesis

Stated honestly, because a strategy memo that cannot be disproven is marketing.

1. **Yurt occupancy underperforms.** The model assumes 45% annual on yurts at $185. Central Oregon winters are hard on canvas structures and shoulder seasons are weak. If yurts run 30% instead of 45%, NOI falls to roughly $164,000 and the case weakens sharply. **Test this first** — pull actual booking data from comparable Oregon glamping operators before committing to Phase 2.
2. **The county reads "private park" narrowly.** If Deschutes staff treat a 30-site commercial camp as too intensive for the EFU private-park allowance, the whole strategy fails at the pre-application conference. **This is a $0 test — go ask them before spending anything.**
3. **Guests won't pay for dry sites in a hookup market.** Bend RV guests may simply route to Scandia or Crown Villa. Mitigant: the dry sites are only 31% of revenue; the yurts carry the model and compete against hotels, not RV parks.
4. **Winter is worse than modeled.** Freeze protection on a central water system serving canvas structures is a real and recurring cost. Consider a deliberate seasonal closure (Nov–Mar) and re-run the math; a 7-month season at higher occupancy may beat 12 months of thin winter.
5. **Access standards bite.** If the county applies an arterial/collector requirement to this use class as it does under DCC 18.128.170(O), the cheap-land premise weakens considerably. **[VERIFY — this is the highest-value single question in this document.]**

## The bottom line

The conventional plan asks: *how do we afford to build what everyone else builds?* The answer is $2M of debt.

This memo asks a different question: *what can be built profitably on the land and under the rules that are actually available to us?* The answer is a 30-site, 10-yurt low-intensity camp on resource-zoned ground — **opening for $365,000, stabilizing near $265,000 of NOI, and protected by the very regulation that makes it unattractive to better-capitalized competitors.**

Version A is the right document if the sponsor is willing to become a $2M borrower. **Version B is the right document if the sponsor wants to own a profitable asset within eighteen months without betting the house on it.**

Both are viable. They are not combinable — the site selection criteria are mutually exclusive, and **the choice of which document to follow must be made before any land is optioned.**
